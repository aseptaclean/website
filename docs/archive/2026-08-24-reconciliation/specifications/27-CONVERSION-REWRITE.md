# Doc 27 §12–15 — Conversion rewrite (DRAFT for owner approval)

Closes register **P11** (service pages ~49% identical boilerplate) and **P12** (no extractable
factual block). One buyer per page. The differentiated opening *is* the buyer segment, so this
fixes duplicate content and voice in the same edit.

**Claims status:** no habitability or safety determination, no decontamination/remediation/
sterilization language, no guarantee of outcome, no price figures except the published
assessment fee, response commitment stated only as "one business day." Every page ends inside
the existing approved boundary blocks, which are unchanged.

---

## 0. The four things that actually drive an assessment request

Everything below is built on these. They matter more than any individual sentence.

1. **Name their situation before naming your service.** People don't search for a service, they
   search for a way out of a situation. The page must say their circumstance back to them in the
   first ten words or they bounce to the next result.
2. **Shrink the first step until it's not a decision.** "Request an assessment" is a commitment.
   "Text a photo" is not. Every page offers both, and the small one is never buried.
3. **Say what the request is *not*.** The single largest silent objection on a form like this is
   *am I hiring someone right now?* One sentence removes it: this starts a conversation, it does
   not book or authorize work.
4. **Tell them exactly what happens next, with a number.** Not "we'll be in touch." One business
   day, reviewed by the owner, and here's what you get back.

## 1. Homepage — routes, does not sell

The homepage's job is to get seven different people into the right door in one screen. It should
not try to sell all seven at once; that is why it currently reads generic.

**H1**
> When you're responsible for a property you didn't choose.

**Lead**
> Estate, hoarding, and severe-condition properties in San Jose and the South Bay — cleared,
> cleaned, and closed out under one signed scope, by one accountable operator.

**Pledge line**
> Nothing leaves the property without your written approval.

**Routing block — "Start where you are"**
Six doors, each one sentence in the buyer's own words, each linking to its page:

| Door | Copy |
| --- | --- |
| I'm settling an estate | A parent or relative has died and the property has to be dealt with. |
| A family member is hoarding | Someone I love needs help and this has to be handled carefully. |

| I manage a commercial space | A project or turnover that needs a defined scope. |

## 2. Estate Cleanout — the executor

**Opening**
> ### You're responsible for a house you haven't lived in for thirty years.
>
> There's a deadline you didn't choose, siblings with opinions, and a garage nobody has opened
> since 2019. The hard part isn't the hauling. It's that something irreplaceable — the deed, the
> photographs, the ring nobody can find — gets decided in an afternoon by people you've never met.
>
> So we decide first, and we write it down. Room by room: what stays, what leaves, what gets
> cleaned, what's excluded. You approve that before anyone touches a box. At the end you get a
> Property Handoff Record showing what was decided, what was cleared, and what was held back for
> you — which is also the document you hand a sibling who asks.

**Extractable block (AI-citation + FAQ schema)**
> **What an executor gets in writing:** a room-by-room scope before work begins, a set-aside list
> of documents, keys, and valuables found during clearing, completion photographs, and a Property
> Handoff Record at closeout.

## 3. Hoarding Cleanup — the family

The only page where the person matters more than the property. **Never mention speed here.**

**Opening**
> ### The hardest part isn't the house.
>
> It's doing this without making someone feel ambushed. Most of what goes wrong on these projects
> happens in the first hour, when someone who isn't ready watches a stranger decide what matters.
>
> We work at the pace the family sets. Nothing is removed that hasn't been approved. Anything
> we're unsure about goes into a labeled clear bag and waits for a decision — it doesn't go in
> the truck. Unmarked vehicles, plain clothing, no signage, and we don't discuss the property
> with neighbors.

**Extractable block**
> **How a hoarding project is paced:** decisions are made before clearing begins, uncertain items
> are set aside rather than discarded, and work stops at any condition requiring a specialist.

## 4. Property Cleanouts / Eviction — the property manager

Highest lifetime value on the list. Repeat buyer, unemotional, wants liability cover.

**Opening**
> ### The unit turns over faster when the scope is settled first.
>
> You need the vacancy closed and you need a record of what was in there — because "my property
> was in that unit" is a conversation you'd rather have with paperwork than from memory.
>
> Written scope before anyone starts. Documented condition at handoff. A record of what was
> removed and what was set aside. We don't decide whether property is legally abandoned — that's
> your call and your process — but we document what we find and hold it for your instruction.

**Extractable block**
> **What a turnover file includes:** approved scope, set-aside inventory of items held for owner
> instruction, completion photographs, and documented exceptions.

## 5. Move-Out / Listing — the realtor

Warmth reads as slow to this buyer. Lead with the date.

**Opening**
> ### Photo-ready by your listing date — or you'll know before you book the shoot.
>
> Send the address and the date. You get back a written scope naming the rooms and the detail
> level, so nothing surprises you the morning of the shoot. If the condition means the date isn't
> realistic, we say that when you ask, not the day before.

**Extractable block**
> **Turnaround:** assessment response within one business day. Scheduling depends on scope,
> access, and current workload.

## 6. Deep Cleaning — the homeowner reset

This buyer has been burned by the word. Name the con.

**Opening**
> ### "Deep clean" means whatever the company decides it means.
>
> You book one, pay more than a regular clean, and the baseboards look the same. The problem
> isn't effort — it's that nobody wrote down what "deep" included.
>
> Ours names it. Cabinet interiors or not. Inside the oven or not. Shower glass, door faces,
> tracks, baseboards, behind what moves — each one either in the scope or out of it, in writing,
> before we schedule. You'll know what you're paying for before anyone arrives.

**Extractable block**
> **What a deep-cleaning scope specifies:** the rooms included, the surfaces in each room, the
> detail level for each surface, and the exclusions.

## 7. The assessment fee — the biggest conversion decision on the site

A paid assessment against competitors advertising free estimates is a real friction point. It is
also a qualifier that protects your time. It converts when framed as **what you receive**, not
what you pay.

**Recommended framing wherever the fee appears**
> Photos are often enough to start, and reviewing them costs nothing. When a property needs an
> on-site walkthrough, the assessment is $195 — and you keep what it produces: a written,
> room-by-room scope with inclusions, exclusions, and a firm price. It's yours whether or not you
> hire us.

Three things this does: separates the free path from the paid one so nobody thinks contact costs
money; converts a fee into a deliverable; and "yours whether or not you hire us" removes the fear
of paying to be sold to.

**Owner decision required:** confirm the $195 is walkthrough-only and that photo review is free.
If both paths are paid, the framing above must change and the conversion cost is materially higher.

## 8. CTA and form microcopy — sitewide

**Primary CTA:** `Request an assessment →` — keep. "Get an Estimate" invites price-shopping
before you've established that you review first.

**Secondary CTA:** `Text a photo` — this is the low-commitment door and it should appear
everywhere the primary does.

**Above the form**
> **Tell us about the property.**
> The city, roughly how big it is, what shape it's in, and when it has to be done. Two or three
> phone photos are more useful than a long description.

**Directly under the submit button**
> This starts a conversation. It does not book work or authorize anything.

**What happens next — beside every form**
> Matthew reads every request himself. You'll hear back within one business day with a real
> answer about whether the job fits and what's needed to price it — not an autoresponder.

**Success state**
> Got it. I'll review the details and contact you about the next step.

## 9. Sequencing

1. Homepage routing block — one edit, affects every visitor.
2. Form microcopy and the fee framing — sitewide, highest conversion-per-word.
3. Estate and Property Cleanouts openings — highest value per lead.
4. Hoarding, Move-Out, Deep Cleaning openings.
5. Remaining service pages on the same pattern.

Steps 1–2 are most of the conversion gain and the smallest edit. Do them first, then wait for
data before rewriting all fourteen pages — doc 27 §30 applies to copy as much as design.
